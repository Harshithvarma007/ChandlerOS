An AI’s ‘Brain’ Isn’t One Thing

New Research Reveals a Shocking Split Between Memory and Logic

We’ve all seen it. You ask an AI chatbot a question, and it recites a perfect, word-for-word quote from a poem. Then, you ask it to solve a simple math problem, and it fails spectacularly. It feels… weird. How can something so “smart” be so “dumb” at the same time? It’s one of the biggest puzzles in artificial intelligence. We treat these giant models like a single “brain,” but what if they’re not?

New, fascinating research from the AI startup Goodfire.ai suggests that we’ve been looking at it all wrong. In a paper that’s sending ripples through the AI community, researchers have provided the first clear evidence that an AI’s “brain” isn’t one uniform blob of digital neurons. Instead, it has at least two major, completely separate processing features: one for memorization and one for reasoning.

And the most shocking part? They found a way to surgically separate them.

The Great Separation: Removing Memory, Keeping Logic

Think of an AI model like GPT-5. It’s built from “training data,” meaning it has read a massive chunk of the internet, books, and articles. From this, it learns to do two main things:

  1. Memorize: Recite exact text it has seen before (like a famous quote or a block of code).
  2. Reason: Apply general principles to solve new problems (like figuring out if a sentence is logical).

We’ve always assumed these abilities were tangled together. To “reason,” an AI must “remember” the rules of logic, right?

Wrong.

The Goodfire.ai researchers discovered that this separation is “remarkably clean.” They found the specific neural pathways responsible for pure memorization. Then, in an experiment that sounds like science fiction, they “removed” those pathways from the model.

The result? The AI lost 97 percent of its ability to recite training data verbatim. It simply… forgot. But its “logical reasoning” ability? It remained almost completely intact.

This is a monumental finding. It’s like discovering that in our own brains, the part that remembers your grocery list is in a totally different building from the part that decides what to cook.

The Twist That Explains Everything: Why AI Fails at Math

So, if reasoning is separate, where do you think basic math lives? In the logic circuits, right? “2+2=4” is the most basic form of logic.

This is where the research gets truly wild. The researchers found that basic arithmetic isn’t in the reasoning pathways. It lives in the memorization pathways.

When they removed the memorization circuits, the AI’s mathematical performance plummeted by 66 percent, even while its logical tasks were untouched.

Let that sink in.

This discovery may finally explain why even the most advanced AI language models are notoriously bad at math (without using an external calculator tool). They aren’t “computing” the-answer. They are recalling it.

As the original article puts it, the AI is “like a student who memorized times tables but never learned how multiplication works.”

For the AI, “2+2=4” isn’t a logical operation. It’s just a fact it crammed for a test, like “the capital of France is Paris.” If you ask it “2+2,” it searches its memory for that exact fact. If it can’t find it, it tries to guess, often incorrectly. This suggests that at their current scale, these models aren’t “thinking” about numbers at all; they’re just acting like giant, fuzzy lookup tables.

How Did They Find This? A Trip to the “Loss Landscape”

To understand how the researchers pulled this off, you need to know about a beautiful concept in AI called the “loss landscape.”

It’s the key to this whole thing.

Imagine you’re trying to tune a super-complex machine with millions of dials (these dials are the AI’s “weights”). Your goal is to make the machine get the right answer as often as possible.

  • “Loss” is just a measure of how wrong the machine is. High loss = many mistakes. Low loss = few mistakes.
  • The “Landscape” is a giant, 3D map showing the “loss” for every possible combination of dial settings.

When an AI is “training,” it’s essentially “rolling downhill” on this landscape, trying to find the lowest possible valley. That valley is the sweet spot where it makes the fewest mistakes.

The researchers didn’t just look at the valleys; they analyzed the shape of the landscape, its “curvature.”

  • Sharp peaks and deep, narrow valleys have high curvature. A tiny change to a dial sends you flying up or down.
  • Flat, open plains have low curvature. You can tweak the dials a lot, and not much changes.

The Hills of Reasoning vs. The Spikes of Memory

Here is the “Aha!” moment. The researchers found that reasoning and memorization create completely different shapes in the landscape.

Reasoning (The Rolling Hills):

General rules — like “if A is taller than B, and B is taller than C, then A is taller than C” — are used by many different inputs. They are shared, robust mechanisms. In the landscape, they look like “consistent moderate curves,” or stable, rolling hills. The researchers write that these directions “add coherently and remain high-curvature on average.”

Memorization (The Sharp, Hidden Spikes):

A single, specific fact — like a unique quote from a book — is only used for that one input. It creates what the researchers call an “idiosyncratic sharp direction.” It’s a sudden, needle-thin spike in the landscape.

Here’s the trick: there are millions of these memory-spikes, and they all point in different, random directions. When you average them all out, this spiky, chaotic mess looks… flat. It has a low-curvature profile.

This was the key.

The researchers used a technique called K-FAC (Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature) to find all the “flat” (low-curvature) parts of the model and all the “hilly” (high-curvature) parts.

They found that the “flat” parts contained all the memorized data. The “hilly” parts contained all the general logic. By removing the low-curvature components, they could erase the memory while leaving the logic-hills perfectly intact.

What This Means for the Future (and Its Limits)

This is more than just a cool academic finding. If this technique can be perfected, it could solve some of the biggest problems in AI safety and ethics.

Imagine an AI model is trained on the whole internet. That means it has memorized private information (like people’s phone numbers or addresses), copyrighted material, and loads of toxic, harmful text.

Right now, it’s almost impossible to make a model “forget” this bad data without completely retraining it from scratch, which costs millions of dollars.

This research points to a future where AI companies could “surgically remove” all that sensitive content. You could, in theory, delete all the copyrighted text without breaking the model’s ability to be a useful, creative tool. You could erase the private data without destroying its ability to reason.

But, we’re not there yet. The researchers are very clear about the limitations.

  1. It’s Not Perfect: They “cannot guarantee complete elimination of sensitive information.” It’s an early step.
  2. Memories Can Return: Other research has shown that “forgotten” information can sometimes be reactivated with just a little more training. The data isn’t gone so much as it’s suppressed.
  3. The “Why” is Still Blurry: We still don’t know why math lives in the memory circuits. Is it truly memorized, or does it just look like memorization to their detection method?

What’s clear is that we’ve just been given a new map to the hidden, alien landscape of an AI’s mind. For the first time, we’re seeing that it’s not one territory, but at least two. And now, we finally have the tools to start exploring them.
