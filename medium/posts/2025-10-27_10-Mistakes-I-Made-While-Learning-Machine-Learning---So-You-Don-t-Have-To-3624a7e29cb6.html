10 Mistakes I Made While Learning Machine Learning — So You Don’t Have To

“At first, I thought Machine Learning was about math. Then I realized it’s about patience.”

When I first started learning ML, I was obsessed with the idea of making models that could “predict the future.” I thought if I watched enough tutorials, everything would magically click.

Spoiler alert: it didn’t.

Over the past few years, I’ve gone from copying code off Kaggle notebooks to actually understanding why things work — but it took a lot of mistakes to get there.

Here are the 10 biggest mistakes I made (and the lessons I wish I’d learned earlier).

1. 🏃‍♂️ Jumping into Models Before Understanding Data

I used to open a dataset and instantly type from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier.

Because who needs EDA when we have models, right?

Turns out, 80% of ML is about understanding your data — not modeling it.

A fancy model on messy data is like trying to race a Ferrari on a dirt road.

Lesson:

Always explore. Ask questions like: What does each feature mean? Are there missing values? Are there outliers?

Good models come from curiosity, not just code.

2. 🎓 Thinking Theory Alone Would Make Me “Good at ML”

I spent months buried in linear algebra, calculus, and probability, thinking that one day, it would all click.

But knowledge without practice fades fast.

Lesson:

Theory gives depth, but projects give clarity.

Start with intuition → apply → return to theory → apply again.

That loop is where growth happens.

3. 📚 Following Too Many Courses at Once

Coursera, Udemy, fast.ai, YouTube — I signed up for everything and finished nothing.

Each course taught slightly different things, and I ended up with fragments of knowledge, not mastery.

Lesson:

Pick one roadmap and stick to it.

Depth beats breadth every time.

4. 🧩 Ignoring the Power of Feature Engineering

At first, I thought ML = choosing the right algorithm.

Then I learned: the magic happens before you even fit the model.

Feature engineering — creating better inputs — can outperform even the best deep models.

Lesson:

Learn to tell stories with your features. That’s what separates good ML engineers from great ones.

5. 📊 Overfitting Without Realizing It

When I saw 99% accuracy, I celebrated like I had just built GPT-10.

Then I realized my test data was basically identical to my training set.

Lesson:

Always split your data properly.

Validation isn’t optional — it’s your reality check.

If your model performs too well, be suspicious, not proud.

6. 🧠 Treating ML as “Code-First,” Not “Problem-First”

I used to chase cool algorithms — CNNs, GANs, LSTMs — without asking why I was using them.

I forgot ML is a tool, not a goal.

Lesson:

Start with the problem statement.

What are you solving? Why does it matter? What metric matters most?

Once you know that, the rest falls into place.

7. 🤖 Ignoring Data Leakage

It took me a long time to understand why my model was “psychic.”

Turned out I was accidentally feeding future information into training.

Lesson:

Data leakage is subtle but deadly.

Always separate what your model should know from what it can’t possibly know yet.

8. 💬 Not Documenting My Work

I would experiment endlessly — and forget what I did two days later.

Reproducibility? Never heard of her.

Lesson:

Treat your experiments like science.

Keep notes, record parameters, and log results.

Future you will thank past you.

9. 📉 Giving Up Too Early

When things didn’t work, I assumed I wasn’t “smart enough” for ML.

But I wasn’t failing — I was debugging my understanding.

Lesson:

Machine Learning rewards persistence.

Every failed model is a clue.

Keep tweaking, testing, and learning — that’s how mastery builds.

10. 🌍 Learning Alone

I tried to do everything solo — no Kaggle, no community, no collaboration.

That was a mistake.

ML is a team sport. You learn faster when you share ideas and mistakes.

Lesson:

Find your tribe — Discords, LinkedIn groups, Kaggle discussions.

Surround yourself with people who are also building.

Your growth will multiply.

✨ Final Reflection

Looking back, I realize these mistakes weren’t failures — they were the foundation.

Each one taught me how to think like a data scientist, not just code like one.

If you’re learning ML right now, here’s my advice:

Don’t aim to learn everything. Aim to understand something deeply.

One project. One dataset. One concept at a time.

You’ll be surprised how far that takes you.
