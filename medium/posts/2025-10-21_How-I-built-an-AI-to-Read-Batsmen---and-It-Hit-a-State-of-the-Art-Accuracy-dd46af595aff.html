How I Built an AI to Read Batsmen — and It Hit a State-of-the-Art Accuracy

I didn’t plan to publish a paper in IEEE Xplore.

In fact, I didn’t even plan to build a “research-worthy” model.

I just wanted my AI to look at a cricket video and say,

“Hey, that’s a cover drive.”

That was it.

No grand vision, no revolutionizing sports analytics.

Just curiosity — the kind that quietly spirals into obsession.

The Accidental Dream

It all began like most late-night projects do — with coffee, chaos, and curiosity.

While doing a literature review (research-speak for “scrolling through other people’s work”), I made a strange discovery:

There was only one proper paper on cricket shot classification using video data.

Just one.

They used a basic CNN — accurate, but slow, limited, and vague about inference speed.

They reported around 83% accuracy, which was impressive… until you realize the model probably took an eternity per prediction.

So I thought — why not try it myself?

No pre-trained weights, no shortcut datasets. Just a clean slate, a laptop, and way too much stubbornness.

The Golden Dataset (With a Lot of Dirt)

Finding the dataset felt like discovering a hidden treasure chest — except when I opened it, it was filled with mud, rocks, and some mysterious bugs.

After days of searching, I stumbled upon a collection of 720p cricket clips, each about a second long — short enough to capture a shot, but not a full play.

At first glance, it was perfect. But as I dug deeper, the imbalance hit me.

  • “Drive” shots: 383 clips
  • Others: barely 150–350 each

The imbalance was like training a model to recognize cats — but showing it ten times more pictures of one cat than the rest.

Still, I moved forward… because the only thing more stubborn than bad data is a data scientist with coffee

Teaching the Model What Matters

Cricket videos are chaotic — multiple players, moving camera angles, crowd motion, scoreboards flashing, and commentary text.

If you show the model the entire frame, it’ll get confused fast. It doesn’t need to care about the bowler’s follow-through or the umpire’s expression.

It only needs one thing: the batsman’s movement.

That’s what defines the shot.

So I decided to isolate that — to strip away everything else and just capture the batsman’s motion.

Enter Mediapipe, Google’s pose estimation library that tracks body landmarks and gives $(x, y, z)$ coordinates for joints.

In theory, it was perfect.

In practice, it was hilarious.

Because when I ran it on the clips… it decided the bowler was the star of the show.

Every. Single. Time.

The model was faithfully tracking the wrong person — drawing beautiful skeletal lines around the bowler while the batsman was just chilling on the side.

When YOLO Saved the Day

After several cups of coffee, moments of self-doubt, and a few “why am I doing this” conversations with myself, I found my savior: YOLOv8 — You Only Look Once.

The name itself felt like motivation.

I trained YOLO to detect the batsman in every frame.

That meant annotating thousands of frames manually in Roboflow.

Frame by frame, bounding box by bounding box, until my dreams were filled with rectangles.

That’s when I realized — this isn’t coding anymore. It’s craftsmanship. 😂

But it paid off.

Once YOLO could pinpoint the batsman perfectly, I cropped the frames and ran them through Mediapipe again. This time, it tracked exactly who I wanted.

I pruned unnecessary points like the nose and fingers, keeping just 15 critical joints — hands, shoulders, knees, and feet — the parts that truly define a shot’s motion.

From each frame, I built numerical embeddings — a compact representation of motion in 3D space.

Now I had something special.

The machine could “see” the player move, not just pixels shifting.

The Accuracy Crisis

I trained my models with excitement.

I expected fireworks — at least 70% accuracy on the first go.

The results crushed me:

Pain.

The architecture was fine. The embeddings were good. But the performance was… tragic.

It felt like crafting the perfect yorker only for it to be smashed out of the park.

The model tanked. My confidence followed.

But like every good comeback story, the next chapter began with a small tweak — SMOTE

The SMOTE Resurrection

I took a step back.

Maybe it wasn’t the model — maybe it was the data again.

And there it was: the imbalance problem, laughing at me from the dataset.

“Drive” had way too many samples, drowning out other classes.

So I used SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique) — a method that generates new, artificial samples for minority classes by interpolating between existing ones.

Basically, it teaches the model to see balance where imbalance exists.

After applying SMOTE, the accuracy leaped to 69.42%.

Not perfect, but enough to show that I was on the right track.

Cleaning the Hidden Dirt

I thought I was done. But the more I inspected, the more inconsistencies I found.

The “miscellaneous” category had clips that didn’t belong anywhere.

Some “slog” shots looked eerily like “drives.”

Even I couldn’t tell them apart without replaying them five times.

If I couldn’t label them confidently, how could a machine?

So I rolled up my sleeves and cleaned it all manually.

Every single clip.

Days turned into nights — and nights into “just one more clip” mornings.

When I finally retrained the model after the cleanup, the result felt unreal.

The 93.4% Miracle

The GRU model hit 93.4% accuracy.

Not just accurate — but blazing fast.

  • BiLSTM: 86 ms per prediction
  • GRU: 40 ms per prediction

That’s less than half the blink of an eye.

The model could classify a cricket shot faster than a human brain could even recognize it visually.

For a moment, I just sat there — staring at the results, not believing them.

All the debugging, the dead ends, the sleepless nights — it all suddenly made sense.

This wasn’t just a machine learning experiment anymore.

It was a reminder that persistence is the algorithm behind every success.

Lessons Learned

What I learned through this wasn’t just technical. It was philosophical.

  • Fancy architectures won’t save you if your data is messy.
  • Cleaning beats complexity, every single time.
  • Patience is the real optimizer.
  • Iteration > Inspiration.

The magic wasn’t in YOLO, Mediapipe, or GRU.

It was in not giving up when everything looked broken.

I didn’t plan to build something state-of-the-art.

I just refused to stop until it worked.

🏁 The Endgame

From a shaky dataset and misidentified bowlers to a state-of-the-art paper published in IEEE Xplore — this journey reminded me what real machine learning looks like.

It’s not glamorous. It’s iterative. It’s frustrating.

But when it works — it feels like magic.

Because in the end, the most rewarding thing about AI isn’t teaching machines to see the world —

It’s seeing yourself grow while trying to teach them.
