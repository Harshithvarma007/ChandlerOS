Hook

Every neural network you’ve ever heard of — GPT-4, DALL-E, AlphaFold, the thing that recommends your next YouTube video — learned what it knows through one algorithm. Not a fancy one. Not a secret. One loop that runs over and over, asking a single question: am I going the right direction?

That’s gradient descent. And it’s genuinely, almost insultingly simple.

Here’s what most tutorials get wrong: they show you the formula, maybe draw an arrow going downhill, and call it a day. You walk away knowing what gradient descent is, but the moment you open a Jupyter notebook, you’re lost.

We’re not doing that. By the end, you’ll know not just what gradient descent is — you’ll know how to write it from scratch in raw Python, speed it up with NumPy, and use it in production with PyTorch. Three layers, all connected.

The Big Picture

Gradient descent is an optimization algorithm. The goal: given a function, find the input that makes it as small as possible.

In ML, that function is the loss function — a score measuring how wrong our model’s predictions are right now. The inputs we’re tuning are the model’s parameters (weights, biases — all the numbers the model adjusts during training). Lower loss = better model. Gradient descent is what drives those parameters toward lower loss.

The ML Training Loop:
Data --► Model --► Predictions --► Loss
                                       |
                               Compute Gradient
                                       |
                               Update Parameters  ◄-- Gradient Descent here
                                       |
                            (repeat until loss plateaus)

And this is genuinely what happens inside PyTorch, TensorFlow, and scikit-learn every time you call .fit() or loss.backward(). The libraries handle the bookkeeping, but the core logic is the loop above.

Real use case: when Google trains its ad ranking model, it has billions of training examples and millions of parameters. A closed-form mathematical solution is impossible at that scale. Gradient descent is the only game in town.

Intuition Before Math

Picture a bowl. A regular cereal bowl. Drop a marble into it from the rim.

The marble doesn’t need a GPS. It doesn’t need a map of the full bowl. It just feels the slope right where it is and rolls downhill. Step by step, it finds the lowest point.

That’s gradient descent. The bowl is our loss function. The marble is our model’s parameters. The lowest point is the minimum — where the model predicts as accurately as it can.

Now here’s the part that trips people up: the marble can only feel the slope where it currently is. It can’t see the whole bowl. It takes one small step downhill, checks the slope again, takes another step. This is intentional — in real ML, the “bowl” has millions of dimensions. You can’t solve it globally. You navigate it locally, one step at a time.

The slope at the marble’s current position is the gradient. It always points in the direction of steepest uphill. So we step in the opposite direction — we subtract the gradient.

Gradient (Personified): I point uphill. That’s my whole personality. I don’t apologize for it.

The one thing our marble gets to control is how far it steps each time. Too large a step and it overshoots the bottom, bounces up the other side, and never settles. Too small and it’ll be rolling forever.

That step size is the learning rate — the single most finicky number in all of ML.

Learning Rate (Personified): Set me too high and I’ll blow up your model. Set me too low and your model will still be training when the sun burns out. I am not easy to work with.

Now… let’s put some numbers to this.

The Math — Step by Step

NERD: What does the gradient actually look like as a number, and how do we use it to take a step?

Let’s work with f(x) = x² — a bowl-shaped function whose minimum is clearly at x = 0. Simple enough to trace by hand, exactly the shape of a loss function we'd want to minimize.

Setup — define everything before we use it:

  • f(x) — the function we're minimizing (think: the loss)
  • x — our current position (think: one model parameter)
  • f'(x) — the derivative of f at x (the gradient, in one dimension)
  • α (alpha) — the learning rate, a small constant where 0 < α < 1
  • n — number of steps we'll take

The derivative of f(x) = x²:

f(x)  = x²
f'(x) = 2x

At any point x, f'(x) is the slope — how steeply the function is climbing right there. Positive means climbing right, negative means climbing left.

The update rule:

At each step, we move opposite to the gradient:

x_new = x_old - α · f'(x_old)
      = x_old - α · 2 · x_old
      = x_old · (1 - 2α)

Wait, what? Every step just multiplies x by the same constant (1 - 2α). That means after n steps from a starting point x_0:

x_n = x_0 · (1 - 2α)^n
For x_n to converge toward zero, we need |1 - 2α| < 1, which means 0 < α < 1. Set α ≥ 1 and the multiplier exceeds 1 — each step overshoots by more than it corrects, and x flies off to infinity. The marble launches itself out of the bowl permanently.

Let’s verify: x_0 = 5, α = 0.1, n = 25:

x_25 = 5 · (1 - 0.2)^25
      = 5 · (0.8)^25
      = 5 · 0.003778...
      = 0.01889...

Twenty-five steps from 5, we’re at 0.019. Closing in on 0.

A few gotchas before we write code:

  • α too high (e.g. 0.6): (1 - 2 · 0.6) = -0.2, so x alternates sign every step. Still converges, just zigzags like it's having a crisis.
  • α ≥ 1: diverges. The model is cooked.
  • Real neural networks: f(x) = x² is a perfect, cooperative bowl. Real loss landscapes have local minima, flat plateaus, and saddle points that make gradient descent genuinely suffer. That's a later episode.

Okay okay, the NERD is happy. Now let’s actually write this.

Layer 1 — Pure Python from Scratch

No libraries. No shortcuts. Just Python and arithmetic so we can see every single operation.

def gradient_descent_scratch(init, learning_rate, iterations):
    x = init  # start at our initial position
    for step in range(iterations):
        gradient = 2 * x                       # f'(x) = 2x - the slope at current x
        x = x - learning_rate * gradient       # x_new = x_old - α · f'(x_old)
    return x
result = gradient_descent_scratch(init=5.0, learning_rate=0.1, iterations=25)
print(f"After 25 steps: {result:.5f}")
# → After 25 steps: 0.01889

The line x = x - learning_rate * gradient IS the update rule from the math. Nothing hidden.

Sorry (I am not) for the extra verbosity below — but watching it actually descend makes everything click:

def gradient_descent_verbose(init, learning_rate, iterations):
    x = init
    print(f"Step  0:  x = {x:.4f},  f(x) = {x**2:.4f}")
    for step in range(1, iterations + 1):
        gradient = 2 * x
        x = x - learning_rate * gradient
        if step <= 5 or step == iterations:
            print(f"Step {step:2d}:  x = {x:.4f},  f(x) = {x**2:.4f}")

Wow. From f(x) = 25 down to f(x) = 0.0004 in 25 steps.

The catch? This is one parameter. Real models have millions. A Python loop over a million parameters on every training step would take hours. This is where NumPy comes in.

Layer 2 — NumPy

Let’s extend the problem: instead of one parameter x, we have a whole vector θ = [θ₁, θ₂, ..., θₙ]. We want to minimize f(θ) = sum(θᵢ²) — same idea, just across all parameters simultaneously.

In pure Python, that looks like this:

# Layer 1 extended — pure Python, one element at a time
def gd_multi_scratch(theta_init, learning_rate, iterations):
    theta = list(theta_init)
    for _ in range(iterations):
        gradients = [2 * t for t in theta]                          # gradient per parameter
        theta = [t - learning_rate * g for t, g in zip(theta, gradients)]  # update each one
    return theta
result = gd_multi_scratch([5.0, -3.0, 2.0], learning_rate=0.1, iterations=25)
print([round(v, 4) for v in result])
# → [0.0189, -0.0113, 0.0076]

This works… but looping over a million elements in Python on every step is painful. Here’s the NumPy version:

import numpy as np   # NumPy — Python's numerical computing library, backbone of all ML
def gd_multi_numpy(theta_init, learning_rate, iterations):
    theta = np.array(theta_init, dtype=float)   # np.array: wraps a list into a vectorized array
    for _ in range(iterations):
        gradient = 2 * theta              # operates on ALL elements simultaneously
        theta = theta - learning_rate * gradient  # updates ALL parameters in one line
    return theta
result = gd_multi_numpy([5.0, -3.0, 2.0], learning_rate=0.1, iterations=25)
print(np.round(result, 4))
# → [ 0.0189 -0.0113  0.0076]

Same result. The explicit before→after for every change:

# Layer 1 (pure Python)
gradients = [2 * t for t in theta]
theta = [t - learning_rate * g for t, g in zip(theta, gradients)]
# Layer 2 (NumPy)
gradient = 2 * theta                       # one operation, all elements
theta = theta - learning_rate * gradient   # one operation, all elements

Why is NumPy faster? Two reasons. First, it calls compiled C and Fortran code under the hood — no Python interpreter overhead per element. Second, it uses BLAS (Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms), a battle-hardened library of vectorized math routines that CPUs are specifically optimized to run. A million-element update that takes seconds in Python takes milliseconds in NumPy.

Same result. Way faster.

Now let’s see what this looks like in the libraries you’d actually use on the job.

Layer 3 — Production Libraries

In production, you almost never implement gradient descent yourself. Libraries handle the update step, but also: GPU memory, automatic gradient computation, numerical stability, and fancier variants like Adam and RMSProp. Let’s look at the two tools that matter.

PyTorch — for deep learning

import torch              # PyTorch — standard library for deep learning research and production
import torch.optim as optim  # optim: every optimizer lives here — SGD, Adam, RMSProp, all of them
# torch.tensor: wraps values in PyTorch's format
# requires_grad=True: tells PyTorch to track this variable for gradient computation
theta = torch.tensor([5.0, -3.0, 2.0], requires_grad=True)
# optim.SGD: the packaged version of our update rule - θ = θ - lr · gradient
# lr is our learning rate alpha
optimizer = optim.SGD([theta], lr=0.1)
for step in range(25):
    optimizer.zero_grad()             # PyTorch accumulates gradients - reset each step
    loss = (theta ** 2).sum()         # f(θ) = sum(θᵢ²), same function as Layers 1 and 2
    loss.backward()                   # compute gradients automatically
    optimizer.step()                  # apply: θ = θ - lr · gradient
print(torch.round(theta.detach(), decimals=4))
# → tensor([ 0.0189, -0.0113,  0.0076])

Same result as Layers 1 and 2. Here’s the explicit bridge back:

loss = (theta ** 2).sum()    ←→  f(θ) = sum(θᵢ²)       [same function, Layer 2]
loss.backward()              ←→  gradient = 2 * theta    [we hardcoded this; PyTorch computes it]
optimizer.step()             ←→  theta = theta - lr * gradient  [our update line, Layer 2]

The big difference is loss.backward(). In Layers 1 and 2 we hardcoded gradient = 2 * theta because we knew the function. In a real neural network with millions of operations chained together, PyTorch computes every gradient automatically. That's the superpower — and the reason it exists.

scikit-learn — for classical ML

For linear models (not neural nets), scikit-learn’s SGDRegressor runs gradient descent internally:

from sklearn.linear_model import SGDRegressor  # scikit-learn's gradient descent linear model
import numpy as np
X = np.array([[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]], dtype=float)
y = np.array([2, 4, 6, 8, 10], dtype=float)   # y = 2x
model = SGDRegressor(max_iter=1000, learning_rate='constant', eta0=0.01)
model.fit(X, y)
print(f"Learned weight: {model.coef_[0]:.4f}")
# → Learned weight: 1.9998

SGDRegressor is running our exact loop from Layer 1, applied to minimizing mean squared error on real data instead of x². The interface hides the loop; the loop is still there.

When Do You Use Which Layer?

Reach for pure Python scratch when you’re learning a concept for the first time or debugging something you genuinely don’t understand. If your NumPy implementation produces wrong output and you can’t figure out why, rewrite it in a slow Python loop where you can print() every single value. You'll find the bug in two minutes. Scratch is also the right choice when you need a custom variant no library supports — a weird regularizer, a constrained update, something experimental.

Reach for NumPy when you’re prototyping or running experiments and need full control over every operation without a full framework’s overhead. NumPy sits in the sweet spot between “slow enough to debug” and “fast enough to be useful.” Most ML research code that isn’t deep learning lives here.

Reach for PyTorch or scikit-learn when you’re building something real. GPU acceleration, automatic differentiation, and a codebase other people need to read all push you toward the library. For gradient descent specifically: if you’re training a neural network, you’ll always use optim.SGD or optim.Adam. If you're fitting a linear model, SGDRegressor does it. You'd only implement it yourself if you were building a new optimizer variant... or writing a blog post about it.

Takeaways & What’s Next

Gradient descent is the loop that runs inside every training algorithm in ML — not optional, load-bearing. Every time you call .fit(), gradient descent is what's running under the hood, taking small steps toward lower loss. The learning rate isn't just a hyperparameter — it's a convergence condition. For f(x) = x², you need 0 < α < 1 or the whole thing diverges. For real networks, finding a good learning rate is still one of the most consequential (and annoying) parts of the job. The three-layer progression isn't just pedagogy: pure Python shows the mechanics, NumPy shows the scale, PyTorch shows what the industry uses.

Without gradient descent, linear regression is just a formula you memorize — the closed-form normal equation that works fine on a small dataset and completely falls apart the moment your data has more than a few thousand features. The math breaks down, the matrix inversion becomes impossible, and you’re stuck. Now that you have gradient descent, linear regression becomes something you can actually build and scale.
