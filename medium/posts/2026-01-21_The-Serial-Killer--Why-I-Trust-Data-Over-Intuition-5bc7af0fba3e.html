The Serial Killer: Why I Trust Data Over Intuition

Most people believe they can spot a monster.

Free read version

We think danger announces itself. That it looks suspicious. That it feels wrong in our gut. We trust that some instinct will fire when something truly bad enters the room.

That belief is comforting.

It’s also wrong.

Because in real life, the most dangerous failures don’t look dramatic. They look ordinary. They hide inside routines. They blend into trust.

Harold Shipman didn’t look like a threat. He looked like a family doctor. He sat in living rooms, drank tea, checked pulses, and reassured worried relatives. He was polite. Familiar. Respected.

And over years, while no one was watching the pattern, he murdered at least 215 patients.

Not in a frenzy. Not in bursts.

Quietly. Methodically. On a schedule.

Nobody noticed…..not because there was no evidence, but because humans are terrible at noticing slow, cumulative signals. We’re good at reacting to explosions. We’re bad at hearing the ticking.

That’s why I trust data.

What Humans Are Bad At Seeing

Imagine a smoke alarm that only goes off once the house is already on fire.

That’s how intuition works.

We notice extremes. We react to headlines. We remember vivid events. But we struggle with gradual change. Ten small deviations feel like nothing. One big deviation feels like everything.

That’s why a doctor who loses one patient doesn’t raise suspicion. Or two. Or five. Each death has a story. An explanation. A reason that sounds reasonable.

Humans are storytellers. We connect dots even when they shouldn’t be connected….. and ignore them when they should.

Data doesn’t do that.

Data doesn’t care about stories. It only cares about frequencies, baselines, and deviation from expectation.

The Pattern No One Wanted to See

David Spiegelhalter, in The Art of Statistics, shows how obvious this becomes once the data is laid out visually.

At first glance, the chart looks almost mundane. Dots scattered across years. Older patients. Nothing that screams “crime scene.”

But stay with it.

The bar charts on the edges quietly tell a different story. The ages repeat. The years repeat. The deaths don’t spread out the way chance would suggest. They cluster. Persistently.

This is what early warning signals often look like in the real world. Not obvious spikes. Just too much consistency where randomness should exist.

On its own, this visualization is unsettling ….. but still deniable. There’s room for excuses. For stories.

Then comes the graph that removes that comfort.

When Noise Turns Into a Signal

This chart compares the time of day Shipman’s patients died with deaths under other general practitioners.

For most doctors, the pattern is what you’d expect. A gentle wave. Deaths spread across mornings, evenings, nights. Messy. Human. Random.

Shipman’s pattern isn’t a wave.

It’s a spike.

A sharp, unnatural peak in the early afternoon. Again. And again. And again.

This isn’t intuition. It isn’t hindsight. It’s a behavioral fingerprint.

Spiegelhalter notes that this pattern doesn’t require sophisticated statistical analysis. You don’t need equations to feel the wrongness of it. You just need to be willing to look at the distribution instead of the individual stories.

Thinking in Systems, Not Snapshots

Most organizations think in snapshots.

Did anything bad happen today?

If the answer is no, we move on.

But harm doesn’t usually arrive all at once. It accumulates.

Think about credit card fraud. One strange transaction doesn’t shut down your card. But ten slightly strange ones in a row do.

Or think about a website. A one-second delay once is noise. A one-second delay every day for a month is a problem.

This is how data scientists think ….. not in isolated events, but in trajectories.

That’s where the Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT) comes in. Not as math trivia, but as a monitoring mindset.

SPRT doesn’t ask, “Was this event bad?” It asks, “Given everything we’ve seen so far, is this pattern still normal?”

Each new data point nudges belief slightly. Most of the time, those nudges cancel out.

But sometimes, they don’t.

Sometimes the line keeps climbing.

The Moment the Alarm Should Have Sounded

This chart tracks how evidence accumulated over time for patients over 64 dying at home or in practice.

Most lines hover safely between the thresholds. Random variation. Noise.

But one line ….. tracking Shipman’s female patients ….. keeps rising.

In 1985, it crosses the outer alert threshold.

Not proof. Not a verdict.

Just a signal saying:

This pattern is no longer consistent with chance.

Shipman wasn’t arrested until 1998.

In those thirteen years, at least 175 more people died.

This wasn’t a failure of statistics. The signal was there.

It was a failure of listening.

False Alarms Are Not the Enemy

No real-world monitoring system is perfect.

Some innocent doctors would have been flagged. Investigations would have happened. Conversations would have been uncomfortable.

But that’s the cost of early detection.

Every applied data system makes trade-offs:

  • Sensitivity vs. false positives
  • Comfort vs. accountability
  • Silence vs. scrutiny

The mistake isn’t triggering an alarm too soon.

The mistake is never building the alarm at all.

How This Shapes How I Think About Data

This story is why I don’t see data as cold or detached.

To me, data is a safety system. A way to catch slow failures before they become disasters. A way to protect people from our own blind spots.

I’m drawn to problems where patterns matter more than predictions:

  • Monitoring systems
  • Anomaly detection
  • Risk and churn signals
  • Model drift and behavioral change

I care less about one-off accuracy scores and more about whether a system notices when reality quietly changes.

Because the most dangerous problems don’t announce themselves.

They whisper.

And the job of a data scientist ….. at least the way I see it ….. is to make sure those whispers don’t go unheard.
