Building an AI Product From Scratch: Why Detox?

Part: 1

Before I start talking up a product I’m building, I should say the obvious part out loud: I haven’t built it yet. Not really. What follows isn’t a launch post. It’s the notebook I’m keeping while I figure this out, published before I know whether any of it works.

So why read about something that isn’t finished? Because if I wrote this after it was done, I’d be tempted to smooth over every wrong turn and make the whole thing sound like it was obvious from the start. Written now, you get the version where I don’t know the ending yet, where the mistakes are still visible instead of edited out afterward. You get two things out of that: something to laugh at when I get it wrong, and something to actually use when I get it right.

So, why build this at all?

Where this started

It starts with my brother watching educational content on YouTube. I was doing my usual “big brother” check to see if he was actually studying, and that’s when I noticed the comment section. The words being used were worrying, and not on some random corner of the internet. This was under education content, the kind kids watch. Which means kids read those comments too, and toxicity like that can affect someone even if they don’t fully understand what it means, maybe especially then.

It’s not just YouTube, and it’s not just text. It shows up as images. It shows up as text embedded in images, which is worse, because most filters never even look there. And it’s not only a kids’ problem. Plenty of adults would also just prefer a less toxic web to scroll through.

That’s what I want to fix: a way for toxicity, in whatever form it shows up, to stop being something we just scroll past like it’s normal.

That’s Detox.

Is anyone actually solving this

Before writing a line of code, I looked at what already exists, because building something that already exists well is a waste of everyone’s time.

There’s a whole category of tools that hide toxic comments, mostly by matching against a keyword list. There’s a separate category for blocking explicit images, usually with decent computer vision behind it but zero understanding of text. There are privacy tools that blur emails and passwords, domain blockers for parental control, and a handful of cloud APIs that big platforms use for moderation at scale.

Each of these does one narrow thing, and does it in isolation. Most of the capable ones run in the cloud, which means your browsing gets sent somewhere to be scored before you see it. The ones that don’t rely on the cloud are usually just keyword lists, which catch the obvious and miss almost everything else. Nothing I found actually reads a page, understands roughly what it’s looking at, and does that on the device it’s already sitting on.

That gap is the actual brief: something that runs entirely on the user’s own machine, no API calls, that understands content well enough to flag it instead of just pattern-matching against a list.

The constraint that rules out the easy path

That one requirement, run locally, no server calls, decides almost everything else about this project.

It means I can’t load a big multimodal LLM onto someone’s laptop that might not even have a GPU. Fine, what about a smaller LLM with a tight, efficient prompt? Also no. Even a “small” LLM is still heavy enough that running it on every page someone opens would slow their whole browser down, not just the tab.

Which means there’s no shortcut here. I can’t wire together whatever’s easiest and call it done. The architecture actually has to be thought through before any of the fun parts start.

Roughly, how it should work

Say the extension is built and someone opens a new page. What should happen?

The page’s text gets pulled out and sent to a model. The model flags whatever looks toxic. The flagged parts get blurred. That’s the whole idea in its simplest form, text only, for now.

Simple to say. Then I remember the actual constraint again: no LLM, and no shipping a page’s worth of text off to a server.

Which means we’re cooked. Well, more accurately, I’m cooked, there’s no “we” here to share the blame with, it’s just me and this laptop.

That “we’re cooked” turns out to be two separate, both genuinely hard, problems. What model can read a page well enough locally to be worth trusting? And how do you get a page’s text to that model without the whole thing turning into a stuttering mess?

Picking the model

Start with the options on the table.

A purely statistical classifier, something like TF-IDF feeding a simple classifier, is fast in the way that doesn’t always help you. It’s Flash Thompson from the Spider-Man movies: hand shoots up, answer comes out instantly, confidence never wavers, and it’s wrong often enough that you learn not to fully trust it. No real sense of what a sentence means once the wording gets even a little clever.

A full LLM is the opposite problem. It genuinely understands nuance and catches what a keyword list never would, closer to Doctor Strange running through those fourteen million futures before he says a word. Correct, eventually. But you did not just casually run fourteen million simulations to answer one question, and neither can someone’s laptop, for every sentence, on every page, all day.

Like a wise person definitely told me at some point, life isn’t black and white, it’s more like fifty shades of grey (kidding, mostly). There’s a middle ground here, and it’s a small transformer, not a full LLM, just the architecture underneath one, trained for exactly one narrow job instead of everything at once. Light enough that I can actually train it without a research budget. Light enough to run on a normal laptop. Still able to read a sentence in context instead of just matching it against a list of banned words.

That’s the model. Whether it’s good enough is a problem for the next post.

One problem already solved, one still open

Getting text to the model efficiently isn’t a new problem, so I don’t have to solve it from nothing. Batches, not single sentences: collect the text on a page, send it over in groups, get classifications back for the group. That part just needs implementing carefully, not inventing.

The part I don’t have an answer for yet: toxicity isn’t one thing. There are different types and different severities, and blurring every flagged sentence the exact same way flattens all of that into a single blunt signal. My current best guess is that the fix lives in the interface, not the model. Different categories of flagged content could read differently, maybe through color, maybe through how strongly something is blurred, so a reader gets a sense of what they’re choosing to reveal before they click. That’s a real open question, not a solved one, and I’d rather say that plainly than pretend I’ve already figured it out.

What’s next

That’s roughly the shape of it: extract the text, batch it, classify it locally, blur what’s flagged, and figure out an interface that doesn’t reduce every kind of harm to the same gray smudge.

The next post is the part I’ve been putting off writing about, because it’s the part I actually have to go build: training the transformer itself, on real data, and finding out how wrong my assumptions above turn out to be.
